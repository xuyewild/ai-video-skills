# Body and Contact

Use this before writing an action passage, to think through how the body moves. This is a checklist for thinking, not something to put in the prompt in full. Only the setup, contact, result, and direction go into the prompt (see SKILL.md).

## 1. Answer five things for every action

"Setup" is what the industry calls the telegraph: the wind-up before a move has to be visible to the audience, or the move looks like it comes from nowhere. It is the same thing as the setup, so it does not get its own line. Put it in the setup sentence.

| Question | What it looks like in the text |
|---|---|
| Where does the force come from | Rear foot pushes off, waist twists left, the beast's hind legs kick |
| What does it touch | Right fist hits the left ribs, left hand clamps the root of the beast's horn |
| Who bears the weight | His right foot is nailed to the ground and holds the weight of both people |
| Where does it go | Slides three steps toward the back left of the frame |
| Where does it stop, in what posture | One knee down, left hand on the ground, sword tip still pointing at the opponent |

## 2. Weight bearing and center of gravity

- A change of supporting hand needs an in-between state: old support → shared or temporary support → new support.
- Punches and kicks: one foot bears the weight, the waist and hips turn first, then the shoulder delivers the hand. An arm swung alone has no weight.
- For a knee strike or a high kick, the other foot must still be on the ground.
- In the air a body cannot change direction from nothing, unless there is an established ability, an outside force, or something it grabs.
- A sharp turn on the ground needs a foot plant and a push-off. On wet or icy ground, write a slip, a lowered center of gravity, or grabbing something in order to stop.
- Height differences: every step of the stairs and every foot placement is really stepped on. State clearly who is above and who is below.

## 3. Contact and force

| Contact type | What must be visible |
|---|---|
| Solid hit | The contact point, the direction and distance the struck side is pushed, and the striker moving forward or recovering |
| Block | The blocking surface (blade, forearm), a half step back under pressure or the blade pressed off line |
| Graze | Slides off along the surface, and both people deviate from their original line |
| Miss | A gap can be seen. The one who struck overshoots and has to adjust their footing to recover. The one who evaded dodged by themselves and was not knocked back |
| Hitting the environment | Contact first, then cracking or denting, then debris last |

- Write being knocked back separately from stepping back on one's own.
- The reaction does not disappear: the person who pushes also sways or has to steady. The one who throws a finisher also needs a reaction under their feet: the ground presses down in a ring, or recoil pushes them back half a step.
- Write force down to the marks and the way they stop: how many steps back, what is left under the feet (a plowed groove, a drag mark in water), and what stops them (bracing on a stone railing, a blade stuck into the ground).
- Parries match one to one: for every strike from the attacker the defender has a matching block, and each block states where it lands and how it turns the strike aside ("the first thrust feints at the face, the second goes for the belly; the axe head tilts and scrapes past the first, the axe handle presses down the second"). Do not write "blocks several moves in a row".
- Repeated hits on the same spot must escalate: each deeper than the last (half an inch → an inch → the whole piece cracks), so the audience sees a guard being broken, not random hitting.
- Borrowing force: using the opponent's forward momentum to kick them over or throw them takes less effort and is more believable than a hard push from a standstill.
- Hits without blood: use a protective qi shield shattering, armor plates flying off, cloth tearing, and the distance of being knocked back instead of bleeding.
- After one exchange, neither person returns to the original stance. The next beat starts from the result of this beat.

## 4. Grabs, throws, lifts

1. Establish the grip point first: grab the collar, clamp the wrist, hug the waist, grab the beast's horn or neck fur.
2. Then break the opponent's center of gravity: pull, push, trip, shoulder them up.
3. Pick only one main force: swing, back throw, trip, toss. Do not stack them.
4. The thrown side reacts according to their ability: grabs the clothing, tucks the legs, braces on the ground.
5. State when the grip is released. After release, the opponent does not accelerate on their own.
6. Write the landing as "body part that lands first → roll or slide → resting posture".

Lifts and riding: who lifts whom, at what body part, whether the lifter's knees are bent. When riding a beast's back, what is held and where the legs clamp.

## 5. Weapon and prop handoffs

- Write the weapon into the subject: "holding a long sword in both hands", not "carrying a sword".
- Lock the holding hand, the blade direction, and the length. Dual-wielded left and right weapons do not cross-swap hands.
- Hand-to-hand handoff or disarm: original holder → the other person touches it → both grip at the same time → the original holder lets go → the new holder grips firmly, and only then does the next action start.
- Throw and catch, or drop and pick up: leaves the hand → flies or lands → caught or picked up → gripped firmly.
- Long-handled weapons: write the tip, the two grip points, and the space swept by the tail end together. Once in close quarters, the grip must change or the person must back off. The weapon does not shorten by itself.
- A broken weapon does not restore later, and nobody grips a part that no longer exists.

## 6. Falls and landings

Loses what supported them → center of gravity moves past the feet → falls → body part that lands first → absorbs it by rolling or stops by impact → resting posture. For a fall from height write three steps: the first body part to land smashes a mark (a ring of cracks under the knee) → slides a distance with the momentum before steadying → leaves wear (the cloth at the knee worn through). The roll goes in the same direction as the original momentum. Hanging: grip point → body swings → feet find a foothold → pulls back up or lets go.

## 7. People with beasts

- Take the relative height from the character sheet first (if a visual bible exists, take its "scale baseline"). If you can attach a scale reference image, do so (for example "the spirit fox stands up to the lead's knee"), and write the distance and contact points of every shared-frame action according to it.
- Beast pounces on a person: hind legs push off the ground → where the front paws land on the person first → the direction the person is pressed down → where the beast's weight rests.
- Person throws a beast: grab a structure that really exists on the beast (horn, edge of the neck armor, foreleg), and say what the person uses to hold up the beast's weight. For heavy beasts, write the person's feet sliding and the force on the ground.
- A small beast on a person: when it lands on a shoulder or an arm, the claws grip the cloth and the body is low.
- The beast's fur, armor, and wings deform on contact and carry on a little after. The person's hand does not sink into the fur.

## 8. Group battles

- **Split the scene and take them one by one**: use corridors, doorways, and bamboo groves to separate the enemies, one opponent per space, which fits "one pair of main contact per beat" (the approach in Blades of the Guardians 2, which hired film action directors to shoot its fights, The Paper, 2026-06-14).
- Each beat picks only one pair of main contact. Everyone else has one thing to do: close in, wait, block the way, recover, exit.
- A change of attacker needs a reason (pushed out of position by the last hit, saw an opening).
- Surrounding does not mean queuing up to be hit, and does not mean everyone brawling at once.
- One local action changes only the few people nearby. People far away change only their route or posture.
- **Enemies do not stand and watch**: those near are knocked back, and those in the middle and far distance fill in right away. Some roll to dodge, some flank and pursue. During a finisher the enemies are still moving (third-party sample, S30).
- **Divide the enemy group by type**: a monster wave or a mob of grunts is not written as one kind of attack. Ranged units spit fire or throw things to suppress, giants hurl rocks and smash the ground to hold the line, small ones pounce and bite up close, and a few elites combine to land one big hit. The lead's responses change to match: cutting through thrown objects, dodging in a zigzag through a bombardment, interrupting the combined attack (S33).

## 9. Limits after injury

- Take the injury from the script or the setting. Do not add to it, worsen it, or suddenly heal it.
- Write it as a functional limit: which side cannot bear weight, cannot grip tightly, cannot lift past the shoulder.
- Compensation must be visible: switching hands, dragging a leg, leaning on a wall or a companion. Actions are half a beat slower, and recovery takes longer.
- Injury changes how a person fights: they guard the injured side when striking, switch hands, or on the contrary get closer, harsher, and more urgent (4 third-party samples). Write it in the next beat, not only as a continuing state note.
- Two people supporting each other: who carries the main weight, the steps must match, and they turn together at corners.

## 10. Vehicles, ropes, and physical triggers

- On a moving cart, boat, or beast's back: write how the carrier moves first, then how the person moves relative to the carrier. When the carrier stops or turns sharply, the person and any loose objects are thrown together in the direction of inertia.
- Ropes: need an anchor point. A rope transmits force only after it goes taut. The swing arc goes around the anchor point.
- Physical triggers of abilities or effects: the action is written only down to "the release, the contact point, the direction, the body's first reaction". How the effect spreads, how bright it is, and when it fades goes to effects design.

## 11. Making action look good: lines, arcs, aftermotion, poses

General craft from the action-design industry (sources are in sources.md under "Action-design craft sources"). When writing the prompt, keep only the visible results.

- **One line of action**: when a move is made the whole body stretches along one main line (rear foot, waist, shoulder, hand, weapon form one arc). This looks better than four limbs each moving on their own.
- **Move on arcs**: write the path of chops, kicks, and turns as arcs ("the sword draws an arc from the upper right to the lower left"). Straight lines are for straight punches and straight thrusts only.
- **Aftermotion lands half a beat late**: the body has stopped, but the hem of the clothes, the hair, the hair ribbon, and the weapon tassel settle half a beat later. Write one sentence at the end of every important action.
- **Readable silhouette**: the pose of a key move opens the limbs and turns sideways to the camera, so the move can be recognized from the outline alone. Do not let an arm block the front of the body.
- **Finishing pose**: after a sequence ends, the character holds the final pose of the move or the closing posture for a beat before moving again ("stays frozen at the end point of the sword stroke"). Distinguish by mode: the stylized mode uses the finishing pose (holds the pose for about a beat, the hem of the clothes settles last). The realistic mode changes it to a "stable end state": stops at the end point of the move and breathes, with no posing (Higgsfield 2026-08-13: realistic fighting is "not dramatic, not wire-work (diao weiya) style posing").
- **Tension and release**: a few fast strikes → a short pause (standoff, breathing, changing stance) → burst again. Not one speed throughout.
- **The weapon is the personality**: a long saber is big and open, a short sword thrusts fast up close, twin hammers are heavy, a fan is light and turns sharply. A character's fighting style matches their personality and weapon, and different characters' moves are written differently.

## 12. Power and impact

- **Timing contrast is stronger than speed**: slow charge-up → the body almost stops → the limb or weapon suddenly goes out. If the strike and the body turn happen at the same time, the move is fast but not heavy. Write it as "left knee lifts and holds for half a beat, then a twisting side kick".
- **The receiver sells it**: the feel of a hit mostly depends on the struck side. The size of the posture change is proportional to the force: head whips away, body folds, both feet leave the ground, breaks something behind them, rolls on landing. If the receiver does not react, the attacker gets no sense of force however hard they try.
- **A hold at the instant of contact**: at the moment of contact write "the two of them, together with the flying sparks, hold for a beat", then write the receiver being knocked away (to be verified: the whole clip may freeze). Do not write frame counts like "hold 2 frames".
- **Rebound and reaction**: after weapons collide, both arms jolt and the blades tremble. Borrow the rebound to press the next move. The one who strikes also has to steady.
- **A small action with a big result** shows strength best: one light cut, and a whole row of bamboo behind breaks at waist height and falls half a beat late. The stronger the attacker, the less effort the body shows: small range, steady torso, expression and breathing unchanged, with the power left to the effects and the receiver (S32; in a finished 2.5 clip a short one-handed horizontal slash followed by a vertical cut was shot this way in the same close-up).
- **Sound**: quiet for a beat before a finisher. Contact is a low dull thud plus one crisp high note (metal, joints), written by material (wood, stone, metal, flesh). Sound follows the impact level: light contact is short and crisp and stops at once, heavy contact is a dull thud layered with low frequency and then one aftershock, a finisher pulls out a beat of silence, then a sonic boom, and finally leaves small sounds such as gravel falling. People make sounds too: panting, a grunt, a clenched-teeth breath, cloth snapping, gravel under the feet.
- **Escalate scene by scene**: the receiver's displacement, the environment damage, and the layers of sound get bigger each scene, and the largest is saved for the last.
- **Little camera shake**: clear matters more than shaking. Light contact gets at most a small jolt, heavy contact a clear dip, and a finisher one or two violent shakes at the peak. Leave shots without shake on purpose in every scene.

## 13. How to arrange a fight

- **Establish positions first**: the first shot is a wide shot, showing the distance between the two, who is on which side, and what usable objects are around.
- **One side leads at any moment**: in one-on-one they take turns leading. With several people, opponents come in one after another and do not all pounce together.
- **Structure (five parts, rising, developing, turning, closing)**: however vague the original fight is, write it as one complete process with highlights and a payoff. The recommended default order: the lead is pressed and beaten first (it can start from probing and feeling each other out, and quickly falls behind) → finds a countermeasure during the fight → the countermeasure turns out not to work much → falls into mortal danger → a key helper or hidden trump card appears, turns the defeat into a win, and escalates to the closing pose. The reversal can use the environment or the opponent's habits. Every move answers "why this one". Even a small conflict must go back and forth and feel like hits, and the opponent must look strong. **The ending point follows the original script**: if the lead wins in the original, run all five parts. If the lead loses, ties, or is interrupted in the original, arrange the five parts only up to "mortal danger" or the original's ending point, and do not change the outcome. A small conflict only needs to go back and forth, not five parts.
- **Starting at a disadvantage looks better**: the lead takes damage first, so the win carries weight. Reactions to pain make the character feel like a person.
- **Look at references first**: before designing, watch the good fight and effects reference films on hand, and learn from their designs.
- **Action and reaction in one frame**: at the moment of contact, keep the attacker and the receiver in the same frame as far as possible, so the audience can see who hit whom.
- **One pair of contact per beat**: write a team attack as a relay, with only one point of contact at any moment (sweep a leg to force the opponent to jump → a partner steps on the shoulder and rises → a palm strike at the highest point).
- **Write in three things and check by three things**: the character's action, the camera path, and how the surroundings react after the hit. If the finished clip is stuck on one of them, revise that description (S29).
- **Open in the middle of the fight**: from the first instant they are already fighting. Write "the lead is already blocking and countering", and do not strike poses first (S29).
- **Moves must connect**: while the last move is closing, the next one has already started, for example "block → slide sideways with the force → counter during the slide". Check whether the person stops and resets the pose between two moves. If they do not connect, cut moves and make one set of attack and defense smooth first (S29).
- **Speed comes from specific ways of moving**: ground-hugging slide, low-altitude boost, flash step (shunbu). Each time, write the direction and the landing point. If you wrote a slide, check whether the person stays close to the ground, and whether the boost direction matches the movement direction (S29).
- **The two opponents fight differently**: one keeps pressing forward and cutting hard up close, the other controls the space while backing off and looks for a chance to press back. This relationship does not change through the whole fight (S30).
- **The official writing principle**: prefer summarizing actions, for example "the two sides engage in close combat", and write detail only for the few memorable actions, without repeating the same action (S1). The author uses this only for supporting fights that are not developed. The main fight is written in full in every beat. So the move-name list (section 15) is used only for the few hits that must be remembered. One third-party sample says "at most 3 actions per second". It is the only one, so treat it as a reference ceiling for actions per second.

## 14. Jargon → images (for design time; write images in the prompt)

The jargon of martial-arts directors and action directors is only for design time. In the prompt, always change it into visible images. Sources are the R series under "Action-design craft sources" in sources.md.

**First decide whether the scene is realistic or stylized** (the old split in Hong Kong martial-arts films, HK01 2018/2025):

| Mode | Image | How to write it |
|---|---|---|
| Realistic | Action stays close to real fighting styles, no leaping into the air, no posing | Write specific moves and real distances. Write "none of the floating feel of wire-work (diao weiya)" |
| Stylized | Made to look good on camera, may leave the ground and strike poses | Write the leap in full: takeoff point → highest point → landing point. In the same show, human-versus-demon can be realistic and god-versus-demon stylized, split by mode (The Paper's review of Fog Hill of Five Elements, 2020) |

| Jargon | Image | How to write it in the prompt |
|---|---|---|
| Northern school (beipai) | Big, open, stretched, tumbling | Large arcs, full-body extension, flips, with the two people far apart |
| Southern school (nanpai) / short strikes | Short close-range strikes with the forearms | The two are within one arm's length, forearms block each other, steps are small |
| Hard bridge, hard stance (yingqiao yingma) | Solid horse stance, forearms colliding hard | Both feet nailed to the ground, forearms collide, upper body does not lean back |
| Bridge hands (qiaoshou) | Making contact with the forearms | Forearm sticks to the opponent's forearm and pushes outward |
| Footwork | Steady steps | State where each step lands ("left foot steps in half a pace") |
| Body shapes (shenduan) | A combination of body postures | Key poses face the camera sideways, recognizable from the outline alone |
| Set routine / timing a routine (taozhao / duozhao) | A pre-choreographed, rehearsed exchange | Design time only. In the prompt, split it into one pair of contact per beat |
| Flesh-to-flesh (daorou) | Raw, real-feeling impact | The receiver reacts big, with one dull thud |
| Selling (mai, the receiver's reaction) | The receiver's reaction decides how strong the audience feels it is | Write the receiver's displacement, folding, leaving the ground |
| Cheating the angle (jiewei) | From some angle the fist and the face overlap in the frame, so it looks like a hit | Camera behind the attacker's shoulder: at the moment the fist overlaps the opponent's face, the opponent's head whips toward frame right. A fallback when clipping keeps happening, to be tested |
| Wire-work (diao weiya) | Flying on steel wires | In a stylized part write the whole leap. In a realistic part write "no floating feel" |
| Tumbling falls (fandie) | Rolling over, somersaults, falling forward | Write the body part that lands first, then the roll, then the resting posture |
| Stiff-body fall (ying jiang shen) | Falling straight and rigid when beaten to near death | Legs straight, falls rigidly backward, back slaps the ground first, kicks up a ring of dust |
| Hair whip (shuai fa) | Whipping the hair to show emotion | After stopping, the hair swings a full circle before it settles |
| Weapon basics (bazi gong) | The basic skill of weapon fighting | Write the weapon hand, the blade line, and use only one kind of attack |
| Stop-burst-stop | A clear image arranged as stop, burst, stop | A beat of standoff → two or three fast strikes → freeze (Bordwell 2008) |
| Wide-shot positioning | Give a wide shot in time to show how far the fight has gone | The first shot is a wide shot showing the distance between the two (Lin Hun on Fog Hill of Five Elements, Jiemian 2023) |
| Reset beat | Insert one separation and breather in 30 seconds | The two back off, each breathes, and they square up again (seedance.tv 2026-08-29) |

**Vocabulary for being hit and going down**: fold (belly hit, body folds up), kneel (one or both knees down), tumble (roll once to shed force), stiff-body fall (falls straight back, back first), slide back (soles on the ground slide backward, leaving marks). Choose from light to heavy. Do not use the heaviest in every clip.

**Action reference video (optional, not used by default)**: generating from a reference video costs more, so do not shoot or generate one just for this. Consider uploading only when you happen to have live-action footage from a martial-arts director, a previs, or a mocap clip, and one key fight keeps coming out wrong. Take only the choreography and rhythm from it, and keep the characters' looks from the asset images (CometAPI 2026-08-10; Kenji Tanigaki on AI letting people test action on a computer first, HK01 2026-06-10).

## 16. Weapon grammar: how each weapon moves and where it goes wrong

Each weapon has its own way of writing the action. The table below is organized from a third-party sample (S31; the author says the clips came out well, with no controlled comparison). In the prompt, write what the "Physics" column means, and turn the "Common error" column into a positive constraint written once.

| Weapon | Moves | Physics (write into the prompt) | Common error → positive constraint |
|---|---|---|---|
| Long sword, long saber | diagonal cut, return cut, side-circling slash, horizontal block, close-in sword press | The holding hand, shoulder, waist, and legs drive the blade together. After a block the blade trembles and the body rebounds | The arm swings alone → "the waist and hips drive the shoulders, back, and arms" |
| Great blade, polearm blade | upward sweep, reverse slash, ground-level horizontal sweep, diving downward chop, dragging blade | The larger the blade, the longer the wind-up and the greater the inertia. If it cannot be stopped, use the inertia to go into the next move | Swung as fast as a short blade → write "the blade is heavy, and after the swing it carries the body through half a turn" |
| Long spear | thrust, shaft sweep, butt reverse strike, return thrust, spinning spear, planting the spear to spin around it | The shaft bends under force and springs back. The spear's advantage is distance | The spear is swung around like a staff → state whether this strike uses the tip, the shaft, or the butt |
| Folding fan | open, close, turn the wrist, horizontal fan, lift with the fan, press with the fan, tap with the fan handle | The number of ribs, the pattern on the fan face, and the opening and closing direction stay fixed. The fan face bends slightly under force and recovers | The fan deforms or turns into another weapon → "the folding fan is always the same one, with a clear opening and closing structure" |
| Meteor hammer, chain weapons | throw out, retract, circle around the body, drag along the ground, lift, horizontal sweep, reverse lash | When thrown the chain goes taut, when retracted it forms an arc, and the spin has centrifugal force. When the direction changes the body's center of gravity changes with it | The chain gets longer and shorter, or passes through the body → "the chain length does not change and always stays outside the body" |
| Fist (with energy) | straight punch, double-fist squeeze, shoulder charge, taking a weapon head-on | A complete force chain through shoulder, waist, and legs. After the punch the arm recoils and the body surges forward | The fist is disconnected from the body → write "the whole person presses forward with the fist" |
| Umbrella blade | twist the handle, open, fold back, diagonal lift, downward spinning slash | Opening and folding the umbrella canopy is a structural change, so state whether it is open or closed now | The damaged canopy has a different shape in every shot → write the damaged structure in the asset setting |
| Giant brush, magic implement | lift the wrist and move the brush, horizontal stroke, left-falling stroke and right-falling stroke, dot, hook, writing in the air | Every stroke is a move, the brush tip is wet with ink, and the flow of the stroke does not break | Swung like a stick → write it as calligraphy strokes |

## 17. How to design a fight (summarized from samples)

- **Decide who presses whom first**: over the whole sequence, who leads and presses forward, who controls the space while backing off, and on which beat they switch. Write one sentence in the character relationship part of the prompt ("the one in black always leads and presses forward, the priestess controls the space while backing off, and from second 20 she presses back").
- **Write a one-line fight chain first**: for example "spear line control → seize the inner circle → repeated spear and sword clashes → black spear powers up → red sword awakens → borrow the spear to spin → fight over the spear shaft → spear breaks → crack in the mask". The last three or four items on the chain are the memorable points (the number depends on the scene).
- **Weapon length decides the style**: long weapons control distance and force the opponent into dangerous range. Short weapons keep seizing the inner circle, press the spear right after a block, and cut back right after a dodge. The two people's styles do not change through the whole scene (section 13, "The two opponents fight differently").
- **The recoil of a collision is the drive of the next move**: when knocked back, use the recoil to surge forward. When thrown, push off a wall and bounce back. No pause after a collision.
- **Use terrain to change the path**: step on a collapsing pillar to change height, use flying debris in the air to change direction, flip along the side of the opponent's weapon and land behind them. Two or three of these in a scene, each written as "ground → thing used for leverage → air → landing point", not as an ordinary double jump.
- **Speed has drops**: fast → faster → one extremely short beat that can still be read → burst. The whole sequence is not at an even speed. During a low-amplitude charge-up the character's motion shrinks, but hair, hem, dust, and flames keep moving.
- **Push the venue along a line**: write an advance order such as "starting spot → old tree → rock → collapsing cliff wall → deep in the ruins", and after each change of position connect a new move at once, never going back to the original spot.
- **Damage escalates**: the environment is intact at the opening → trees broken and rocks shattered in the middle → all ruins in the later part → the last hit pushes it to the limit. What is already broken does not restore.
- **Destruction ladder** (third-party sample: smash floor tiles and break a pillar → cut a corridor pillar so the steps collapse → a side hall falls → the main dome and the statue collapse, with each level matching a move one intensity tier higher): at the start of a scene list 3-4 levels of destructible things, in words and not in codes. The end frame of every fight video states "which level has been reached and what is already broken", and the next one inherits it as is, with no rollback and no automatic restoration.
- **Round ledger (made at design time, not put into the prompt)** (summarized from several third-party two-person fight samples, for arranging rounds across a whole scene, to be verified in finished clips):
  - List two columns for each round: the attacker's shape (point, line, fan, wall, ring, net, clamp, close-in heavy chop) and the defender's response (hard block, absorb, dodge, slip through the gap, deflect, borrow force and counter-tap). Adjacent rounds do not repeat, and a shape appearing a second time must escalate.
  - The initiative changes hands every 2-3 rounds. The beat of the change has a marker (the receiver's expression or posture changes), and the next move comes straight on with no charge-up. Spread out the result levels: blocked → grazed (cuts off a sleeve piece, a strand of hair, a robe belt, tallied with clothing and not wounds) → hits a non-vital spot → repeated hits → knocked flying.
  - How to write the crisis beat: the heaviest hit of the whole scene → weapon knocked loose or almost loose, one knee down or on the ground → armor cracks, breath comes out white → a close shot of a resolute gaze → the next beat rolls to take the weapon back and counters at once (ideally hitting a point planted earlier). In the sample this beat sits at about the midpoint of the whole sequence, and in the five-part structure it comes later. Set it by your own structure, to be verified.
  - Counter from the source of the opponent's advantage: seize the inner circle of a long weapon, remove the resistance of a heavy weapon so it overshoots, hit the same spot three times until the weapon cracks and breaks, destroy the force fulcrum. Write what the opponent relies on to hold the upper hand first, then which one this move takes apart.
  - Change the contact medium in close quarters: with the weapons pressed together at the closest range, the next round uses the hilt, elbow, knee, shoulder, or back. Change it at least once per scene, in the closest-range round, and that round does not use the blade edge.
  - The state of weapons and armor also escalates (blade turning red, spear shaft bent → cracked → broken, shoulder armor half broken). Write them as in-setting stages, and what breaks does not restore. Whether a gradual change is stable on a video model is to be verified.
  - Distance and height also draw a curve (ranged → close → clinch → separate; ground → air → landing). After close combat and before the final move you can arrange one separation and face-off. This beat has no shake, and only hair, hem, and debris move, and then they charge up and rush each other. Borrow only this beat, not the sample's ending of "evenly matched, both release a finisher at once".
  - Set the formation → enter the formation → break the formation: one side leaves lasting traces while moving to form a net or a cage, the other enters and is trapped, and the next round breaks out through the gap left when the formation was set. Three rounds count as one "turn", placed between the second and third parts of the five.
  - Not adopted: the sample's ending of "a draw, each leaves" (a fight where the lead wins in the original runs all five parts. If the original is itself a draw or a defeat, close at the original's ending point, see section 13), and "the terrain is rewritten by both sides in turn" (only fits elemental battles).
- **A solo showcase has no opponent**: use scene objects as impact points (a stone arch, a floating rock pillar, distant stone pillars), and every hit lands on something visible.
- **Every hit in a combo changes something**: change the struck body part, change the shape of the opponent's defense (round shield → hexagon → diamond net → hemisphere), and change the camera direction. Only the last two hits on the same spot break through (third-party sample: seven hits in five seconds, an eight-punch combo in one second, both written this way).
- **The person does not stop after releasing a move**: the one who made the move acts again on the next beat, rushes forward following the blade qi, finishes off what was missed, turns to the next target. A summoned thing starts fighting as soon as it forms, and does not stand and watch (third-party samples, 2 of them).
- **Chain cuts match dodges one to one**: each cut is paired with a dodge, leaving an afterimage in place, and the next cut misses and slices that afterimage apart (third-party sample).

## 18. People against giant beasts

- A giant beast's attacks have weight and the person's dodges have speed. Every strike of the giant beast comes with damage to the scene (claws smash flagstones, a tail sweep breaks trees).
- When the person hits a part of the giant beast: wing, claw, side of the neck, eye, one hit on one spot, and the struck spot states the direction of deflection ("the left wing is cut and swings aside").
- The giant beast uses the scene to counter: grabs a broken tree and swings it sideways, grabs a boulder and smashes, rams a cliff wall down.
- The size difference comes from the camera: alternate an extreme wide-angle sense of oppression with close-ups of parts (the giant beast's eye, the giant fist). When the person moves on the giant beast, state where they climb on and along what they run.
- The giant beast being hit: the body turns aside or steps back, fur and flames are lifted by the impact, the ground under its feet cracks.

## 19. Seven questions per beat, redesigning the fighting style, and error-prone contact

Source: summarized from the rework records of a batch of finished AI comic-drama clips. The experience is that fight scenes and effects scenes go wrong most easily, and the fighting style in the original script is not necessarily good, so it can be thrown out and redone. The writing below is a design method. No controlled experiment has been done, and it does not guarantee a higher success rate.

**Authorization boundary**: the "fighting style" in the original script (who strikes first, what move, where it is caught, how it is countered, what terrain is used) can be overturned and redesigned. What is **not** touched: who wins and where they end up (section 13), injury facts and the injured side, character arcs, and dialogue locked by the author. Mark a redesign "fighting style redesigned" in the delivery notes, with one sentence on which question the original could not answer and how the new style answers it.

**Answer seven questions first for every key contact beat** (answered at design time. The prompt only writes the visible results. The five things in section 1 are a refinement of questions 4-6):

| # | Question | Common finished clip when it cannot be answered |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Who wants to achieve what: push back, interrupt, take position, break away, or knock down | Both sides hold poses and wait for each other's move |
| 2 | How far apart are they, where is each, and **which way each faces** (write facing separately, not only left and right) | Turns the back to the enemy, punches at empty air |
| 3 | Which hand, foot, or weapon starts, and where is the support point | Empty swing, teleport with no setup |
| 4 | What spot is hit; what the defender uses and where it is caught | Two fists meet, the fist path cannot be read |
| 5 | After contact, where does the force go and who loses balance | Nobody moves after the hit |
| 6 | How the struck side is displaced, where they land, and what stops them | Suddenly flies away, landing point has no source |
| 7 | How the defense or counter uses the opening left by the last beat, and how it connects to the next clip | Resets the pose every beat, replays the setup |

- **Back and forth is not mechanical symmetry**: an action must change the other side's situation, and the other side must make an effective response. Do not arrange "you punch, I punch" just to fill a count.
- **Golden light does not replace contact**: write where the body or weapon really touches first, then what the energy does there.

**Additional error-prone contact** (from finished-clip records, not covered in sections 1-10):

| Type | Must state | Hard errors recorded |
|---|---|---|
| Trampling, area attacks | Who and how many are under the foot or in the area; who is outside the area and where they stand; the foot landing spot | The lead, who should be outside the area, gets mixed into the crowd being trampled |
| Height actions | Starting height and support, leaving the support, path, landing point, height reference object | A ground character is suddenly in mid-air, height relationships reversed |
| Weapons (addition) | Write the weapon type into the subject and check it at every cut | Staff turns into a blade |
| Group battle (addition) | Write the lead and the background crowd as separate identities | A clone of the lead appears in the crowd |

**When something goes wrong, layer it first. Do not blame length and sharpness first**: the attribution "480P compute was not enough, so the fight went wrong" has no controlled experiment behind it in the current records. Check in this order: whether the space conflicts, whether the contact is clearly written, how many competing tasks are crammed into one segment, whether dialogue takes up the action time, whether assets are bound wrongly. Failing after two or three exchanges is a different thing from cramming a group battle, long dialogue, a transformation, a finisher, and a fall into ten seconds. If you split, split by complete cause-and-effect segments (segment A has the attack and block, segment B picks up the displacement after the block and counters), and do not break at the instant of contact. The end frame of the earlier segment and the start frame of the later segment must match, with no pose reset and no replay of the setup.

## 20. Writing techniques learned from excellent AI fight shorts

The following comes from a shot-by-shot breakdown of a batch of AI shorts with very good fights, effects, and camera work (the sources are not public, timecodes omitted). These are techniques not covered in the earlier sections.

1. **A combo without cuts**: write it in one shot from one camera position, about half a second per strike (rise to the peak and hold for an instant → chop down → hit → immediately rise again), with the impact point landing in the same spot of the frame each time. The last strike is raised higher, leans back deeper, and holds longer, as the closing heavy blow. Write three layers for each strike: sparks at the contact point, a dent or flying debris where it was hit, and the attacker rebounding from the recoil.
2. **A pursuing strike lands half a step off**: every strike has a landing point (stuck in the ground, sparks, a ground crack), and the landing point is always where the target has just left. The pursued side dodges in different ways (roll → jump → crawl low → jump again), and does not use the same one repeatedly. In the end, a symmetrical composition seals the escape (a cage of swords, surrounded on four sides).
3. **Fast and slow within one move**: a fast charge (background blurred sideways, the subject clear) → one shot pulling back to show the distance → meets the attack → the contact point written slow for about 2 seconds (cracks and debris increasing, squinting eyes, a wrinkled nose, clenched teeth on the face) → the shattering flashes by → pins the opponent down and stays still for about 2 seconds → burst. The slowest goes to the contact point, the fastest to the shattering.
4. **The climbing route of the small against the large**: slip between the legs → grab fur or armor seams and climb up → stab a weapon in as an ice axe → stand up on the back → smash. Every step lands on a specific part of the opponent's body.
5. **One weapon, two uses**: a chain wrapped around a building as a swing rope, wrapped around a horn to pull oneself across; a staff stuffed crosswise into a mouth to jam the opponent's charge-up; the staff tip dragged on the ground throwing sparks to show weight.
6. **Being knocked away gives distance**: after the knockaway cut to a very wide shot, with the person only a small dot in the distance, then a flip in the air and a shattering landing, one shot each. A companion can catch them in the air.
7. **One action makes the whole environment respond**: with one stomp, soil and turf heave up like a wave over a whole area, and the enemies are swept away like debris.
8. **Damage that accumulates visibly**: swords stick into a body one after another, more and more. The finishing hit is a dozen times the size of an ordinary attack, with a shockwave ring at the landing point. More and more cracks on a shield show that it is about to give out.
9. **Taking a hit with the body, a three-direction combined attack**: a companion blocks in front of a beam with their body. Three sides strike the same point from three directions at once, and a ring bursts open at the center.
