# General Color Design Reference (across projects and genres)

Good color raises the texture of a whole film by a grade. This file is general color knowledge collected online, on how good films and directors use color. Any project and any genre can consult it. How a specific project chooses goes into that project's visual bible (`## Color`, and the optional `## Palette system`); this file does not decide for a project.

**Sources and reliability**: the note in brackets after each item is its source. First-hand = interviews with the cinematographer, production designer or colorist themselves, in American Cinematographer (ASC), British Cinematographer, Filmmaker and similar. Relayed = what teaching sites, reviews and analysis sites (StudioBinder, No Film School, colorculture and others) say. Inference = the AI extrapolated from a source; nobody said it directly. The meaning of a color varies with genre and culture, and there is no universal table (Christopher Doyle's own words: "It's not as simple as Storaro and other people claim."; No Film School: no single correct color goes with a given emotion). The "conventions" below are the default readings audiences have been trained into, not rules; going against a convention needs a reason. None of these items has been checked one by one by producing video with a video model.

## 1. Decide the color system before you start

1. **First write this film's "color ideology"**: what each color stands for in this film, who uses it, and when it appears. Write it down, then carry it out (Storaro writes an ideology for every film first, relayed in ASC; before the grade of Dune: Part Two, FotoKem made a colour bible of about 50 minutes months ahead, British Cinematographer; The Shape of Water production designer Austerberry: palette work comes before other steps, first-hand). The mapping table is valid only inside this film.
2. **Main, secondary and accent colors roughly 6:3:1**: the main color sets the scene's identity and mood, the secondary supports, the accent is small in area and often carries meaning (No Film School and Wolfcrow's 60-30-10, relayed). This is a starting point, not an iron rule. A near-two-color or near-monochrome scene also works, but say who is the main color and which one is the only accent.
3. **Reserved color**: one color carries only one meaning, and the less it is used the more weight it has (in the black-and-white Schindler's List only the little girl's red coat is in color; in Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon the Green Destiny sword appears with one green lamp used in one place to make a faint glint in the eyes, Peter Pau ASC, first-hand; in Your Name. the red cord is the visual anchor in pure primary red, relayed). When the same color has a non-reserved use, tell them apart by material and by lowering value (inference: gold light and the gold of metal are not the same thing).
4. **One color set per world, space or faction**: reality cool, fantasy warm, with the two sets seeping into each other at the climax (Pan's Labyrinth, cinematographer Navarro, first-hand); cold concrete against a warm golden apartment (The Shape of Water, first-hand); the land of the living soft, the land of the dead exploding with color (Coco, lighting cinematographer Feinberg, first-hand); each place has one color identity, transitions follow the order of the journey, and the same people share one treatment (The Lord of the Rings, Lesnie, colorist Peter Doyle, ASC, first-hand); each planet has one physically plausible set of light and color, with costumes changed to follow (Dune, first-hand); the rich home warm gold and bright, the poor home cold, dark and fluorescent (Parasite, relayed).
5. **Character identity colors**: the lead gets the most eye-catching color and the strongest contrast, supporting characters are softer, background people the most restrained (cg-wire, relayed); each character has a fixed palette, so they can be told apart at a glance even in one frame (Spider-Man: Into the Spider-Verse, art lead Justin Thompson, relayed by Cartoon Brew); identity colors must hold up in fights, night scenes and spell light (The LEGO Batman Movie's grade keeps identity colors such as the eyes and cape, relayed). A check: put the character on a black-and-white version of the background, and if they blend in, adjust (cg-wire, relayed).
6. **Color comes from the scene and props**: first see what colors the real locations and materials can give, then assign them to the story (Doyle on Hero: "you cannot change the color of the lake", first-hand); color is made in art direction, costume and lighting, and the grade cannot save a weak palette (Deakins: Blade Runner 2049 "everything was done on set"; Zhang Yimou's Shadow refused a one-click computer desaturation and made the ink-wash feel with real objects and costumes, first-hand).
7. **Non-film media can be a reference**: Chinese watercolor and ink wash (Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon; Shadow; first-hand), comics (Spider-Man), painters (Pan's Labyrinth refers to Goya; Apocalypse Now refers to Rousseau's jungle paintings), a historical image style (The Greatest Showman refers to 1950s Technicolor musicals, cinematographer McGarvey, first-hand).
8. **A list of real light sources for the era and world**: first list which light sources exist in this era and this world (tungsten, fluorescent, sodium lamps, neon, candles), and choose colors only from the list (Green Book, cinematographer Sean Porter, relayed by Filmmaker).

## 2. How color moves with the story (color script)

- **Color by section, one main color set per section**: Hero's red, blue, white, green and yellow sections (Doyle, first-hand); The Ballad of Buster Scruggs gives each story a very narrow palette (Delbonnel, first-hand).
- **The film's "amount of color" is an arc**: The Shawshank Redemption goes from gray monochrome to dazzling color, and the prison's tungsten yellow gets thicker as the story goes on (Darabont, Deakins, ASC, first-hand); in Up, after Ellie dies Carl's life loses its color, and the new companions bring color back one by one (color script, relayed). Loss = desaturation, return = color coming back; this can be reused in any structure from sadness to healing.
- **The overall curve of a long series**: the first two Harry Potter films are bright and warm, the third is cold gray leaning teal, then darker and greener (the villain's power spreads), the finale is high contrast, and the epilogue returns to brightness echoing the first (No Film School, relayed; for the sixth film Delbonnel wanted "dark and soft grays", treating the school as a dark character).
- **A turning episode changes the whole grade**: it announces "the story has changed" (Prisoner of Azkaban, relayed).
- **A warm breather inside an oppressive stretch**: in The Shawshank Redemption, drinking beer on the roof, bathed in golden light (relayed).
- **Weakening and recovery**: desaturate by color separately, protect skin tones and press down the environment, and the colors return after the spell is lifted (The Two Towers, Rohan, ASC, first-hand).
- **Changing worlds raises saturation and contrast**: in Spider-Man: Into the Spider-Verse, entering the spider-universe is brighter and more saturated, and in emotional moments the whole frame changes color (relayed).
- **Deliberately avoid one strong color**: Wong Kar-wai's films before In the Mood for Love have no red (Doyle, first-hand), so it carries weight when it appears; strip all color only in very rare moments (the only black-and-white scene in The Grand Budapest Hotel, relayed).
- **Day and night are two sets of hues, not just a change in light and dark**: in Mad Max: Fury Road, desert red and yellow against blue sky, and night in blue and silver (grading commentary, relayed); light-color transitions within a scene speak of time and fate (in The Last Emperor the red sun fades and the moon rises, ever bluer, Storaro ASC, relayed).
- **A highly saturated world needs quiet sections**: use blue and purple to rest the eye (Coco, Feinberg, first-hand).
- **Each time layer has its own color character**: memory highly saturated, reality faded (The Grand Budapest Hotel, relayed); the same place red in its heyday and green-orange in decline (Dwell's report on production designer Stockhausen, relayed).

**Seeing how a film's color moves**: the movie color page on screenmusings.org (screenmusings.org/color/movie-color-barcodes, titled "Movie Spectra and Color Schemes") gives each film a color spectrum. The screenshots are processed frame by frame in order, and each frame's dominant color is placed side by side and then followed by the next frame (the page's own words: "processed in sequence"). So you can roughly see the sections, the arc and where the reserved color appears. Click one vertical color bar to enter that color's page and see that scene's stills and how the same color is used in other films. About 300 films; the color library has about 20,000 screenshots from 192 films, mostly taken by hand by the site owner (the owner's own account; a personal site, incomplete coverage, not a first-hand source). Use: before you set a color script, look at the spectra of similar films to confirm how "one main color set per section" and "the arc of color amount" roughly divide in real films. Record what you see as "inference". The site states no copyright or terms of use, so look only and do not use the screenshots: do not download them into the project, do not upload them as reference images, do not write them into generation prompts, and do not use them as a source of color values. If a film is not included or the page will not open, skip it; it does not block anything.

## 3. How to color a single frame

- **Separation before saturation**: when a frame looks flat, do not add saturation first. Make shadows cooler and highlights warmer to pull out depth; push strongly saturated colors darker at the same time to get "color density" (No Film School's three grading tricks, Darkroom, relayed).
- **One or two hues per frame**: a main color plus one contrast color, with a third color used sparingly (Hero, inference). A rough author measurement: in a set of 28 film lighting demonstration frames, the two largest hues made up about 86% of colored pixels (inference, small sample).
- **Let only one place per shot carry high saturation**: the subject or the accent, with the rest yielding to the main color (several sources combined, inference).
- **Separate the subject from the background on at least one axis**: hue (cool/warm or complementary), saturation (subject higher than background), value (bright against dark); if that is not enough, add rim light, shallow depth of field, haze or dust for layering (Robert Morton, cg-wire, relayed).
- **Skin tone**: keep natural skin, push the environment to the complementary cool side to set the person off; strong color casts only under deliberate light (Frame.io, Provideo grading tutorials, relayed); for night blue choose a "classic blue" that does not lean purple, friendlier to skin (Rivendell at night in The Lord of the Rings, ASC, first-hand); for two leads with different skin tones, first settle one ambient light that works for both (Green Book, relayed).
- **Shadows**: for most genres use colored dark tones in the dark, not pure black (Ghibli color designer Michiyo Yasuda, relayed by Animation Obsessive); noir and thrillers are the opposite, they need true black to hold the main color (The Shape of Water cinematographer Laustsen: "The black is going to be really black."; Se7en used silver-retention processing to keep blacks, Khondji ASC, first-hand). Choose by genre.
- **Atmospheric perspective**: the farther away, the paler, lower in contrast and closer to the color of the air (usually cooler), with the strongest contrast in the foreground (Draw Paint Academy, relayed).
- **Light sources are the palette**: the colors of billboards, screens, lanterns, glowing plants and spell light change with content; fog, smoke and dust let colored light spill (Blade Runner 2049, Deakins, first-hand; in Avatar night scenes bioluminescent plants light faces, relayed; Coco's colored lights and candlelight).
- **Protect one small color inside a large single-color area**: Dune's grade protects the blue eyes of the Fremen (British Cinematographer, first-hand).
- **Vertical phone screen**: color must read at a glance and guide the eye, and separate the character from the background; when a scene has many colors, lower the environment's saturation and let the character be the emotional center; when the same scene returns, change the palette and contrast (interview with a vertical-drama colorist, The Source, relayed).

## 4. Look up by genre

| Genre | Common practice | Clichés to avoid | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Horror, thriller | Low value, cool base (blue, green), red kept for violence and warning; the opposite approach is bright daylight with no shadow to hide in | Red emergency lights, night-vision green, 2000s blue nights as the only tool | Suspiria (1977) multicolor floods; Midsommar daylight horror |
| Mystery, crime, noir | First set the density of the black and keep detail, then decide how much color to pull out, and put needed color back with art direction and costume | A modern imitation's all-warm-brown look | Se7en (Khondji, first-hand); Chinatown; Drive neon pink-purple with warm gold nights |
| Romance | Choose one main color that carries the relationship, and change it as the relationship moves | Pink-orange everywhere by default (inference) | La La Land blue-green, pink, purple (Sandgren, first-hand); In the Mood for Love red against green |
| Comedy, family | High value, low contrast, medium-to-high saturation; separate saturation by era or space so the whole film is not one intensity | High-saturation primaries easily turn cartoonish | The Grand Budapest Hotel; Coco |
| Sci-fi, cyberpunk | One color identity per location; neon and screen light for warm/cool contrast | Orange and teal as the default | Blade Runner 2049; The Matrix green (Pope ASC, first-hand); Dune |
| Fantasy, xianxia (immortal-hero fantasy), magic | Not always high saturation: desaturate as the base and let one or two hues carry meaning; plan magic light separately | A whole screen of cyan-blue with gold particles and no layers (inference) | The Lord of the Rings; Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon low-contrast desaturated ink wash (Pau, first-hand); Hero |
| Action, wuxia (martial arts) | Two paths, saturated and theatrical or desaturated and soft-lit, matching the story's aesthetic; environment cool on one side and warm on the other, skin tone in the middle | A desaturated and dirty apocalypse grade | Mad Max: Fury Road; Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon |
| War | Low saturation, medium-to-low value, with only one or two meaningful color outlets (blood, fire, one garment) | One uniform gray-blue for the whole film (inference) | Saving Private Ryan chose color for the red of blood, desaturated about 70% (Kaminski ASC, first-hand) |
| Period, costume drama | Period feel comes from sourced light (candles, lanterns, windows) and a restrained palette | A one-click warm-brown period filter (Gordon Willis's criticism, relayed) | Barry Lyndon candlelight; Raise the Red Lantern red lanterns against gray walls |
| Youth, campus | Give color to characters and world, not one unified color; dreamy high saturation or memory-like plainness both work | The same Japanese-style light blue and white everywhere (inference) | Lady Bird memory-like color (IndieWire); Euphoria |
| Family, realism | Natural, medium-to-low saturation; use one pair of cool and warm to speak of class or space | Realism = a whole film desaturated gray (inference) | Parasite; Green Book |
| Urban, "domineering CEO", revenge-payoff drama | No dedicated norm found. Borrowable: color moves with the change of identity (Joker's cool dusk blue against warm hopeful light, cinematographer Sher, first-hand; Breaking Bad), cold gray for humiliation, one single high-saturation color added for the counterattack (inference) | — | Kill Bill bright yellow |
| Animation | Restrained, mid-tones, transparency, strong color only in places; a fantasy creature's color should stay close to real objects to be believable (the Catbus is not pink); dark areas in colored darks | Full high saturation with one set of warm light everywhere (inference) | Ghibli (Michiyo Yasuda); Makoto Shinkai's Your Name., cool Tokyo and warm Itomori |

## 6. Eras, textures and popular grades

- **70s warm copper**: The Godfather's unified warm tone of copper yellow-red, built on contrast, not on darkness (British Cinematographer, relayed); modern imitations of the warm-brown treatment rarely succeed.
- **80s neon**: pink-purple neon with warm gold nights (Drive), uncorrected fluorescents mixed with colored gels.
- **Film, nostalgic feel**: write specific texture, not a filter name: overexposure, push processing, print-stock simulation (Lady Bird's cyan in the highlights and lavender in the blacks, IndieWire), grain, lifted blacks; do not write only "retro".
- **Bleach bypass, silver retention**: pull color and keep the black, metal and water looking like mercury (Saving Private Ryan's ENR about 70%, Kaminski says its real effect is in highlights and color, ASC, first-hand; Se7en).
- **Orange and teal**: complementary contrast makes skin pop, so it is popular, and so it has been criticized as "Hollywood's teal-and-orange-ization". Treat it as an option, not a default: when you use it, say which side is environment and which is skin, and lower the saturation or limit the area (Mad Max: Fury Road's approach).

## 8. How to write color in video prompts (Seedance 2.5 as the example)

No section dedicated to color was found in Seedance's official prompt manual. The following comes from official analogies (Sora, Veo) and third-party experience (the Kling blog and others):

- **Write three to five color anchors per scene**, put them in the "keep consistent" part, and reuse them word for word across the prompts of the same scene (Sora official cookbook: "Naming three to five colors helps keep the palette stable across shots."; third parties such as Fliki). Example (a fictional academy day scene): "Colors for this scene: cream marble, navy uniform, pale blue sky, warm white highlights, with cyan-white only on the amulet."
- **Write the color right next to the thing it belongs to**, one color per thing ("the boy in the navy uniform", "the cyan-white amulet on Lin Zhou's wrist"), the thing taking up most of the frame first. Many objects with many colors is a weak point of models, and colors easily bleed onto other things (the Color Bind paper; Midjourney community experience, relayed).
- **Write where color comes from**: the color of the light source plus the medium ("warm white sunlight from the window on the left", "the orange of the lantern scattered by thin mist"), and the color of the clothing and the scene itself. Do not write "XX color-tone filter" or "cinematic grade" (several sources, relayed).
- **Write shadows as colored dark** (deep navy, dark teal-gray), not pure black, except for noir genres (Fliki, relayed).
- **Use the same sentence for clothing color in every prompt**, with reference images locking the color; one outfit per sequence; solid colors and few patterns are more stable (seedance.tv, community).
- **Color values (HEX) are not the main tool**: whether Seedance follows them was not found (FLUX's official docs say HEX works and should be bound to a specific object, which does not apply to Seedance). The visual bible rules that color values stay out of the video style block.
- Write what you want to avoid (color drift, a color wash over the whole frame) as what you want first ("nearby characters keep their original skin and clothing colors") (Runway and Veo officially favor positive wording).

## 9. Cross-checks

- In this scene, whom does this color speak for? If you cannot say, do not add it (No Film School: no single correct color).
- What are the main color, the contrast color and the accent? Is the accent in only one place?
- Has the reserved color been used up by something else?
- Are subject and background separated on at least one axis of hue, saturation and value?
- Can you see a difference in color between two adjacent scenes? Does the whole episode have a color curve?
- Is skin natural? Are spell light and neon on faces deliberate?
